Converting Cumulative Returns to Annualised Returns

Cumulative returns are converted to annualised (compound annual growth rate) using:

Annualised Return = (1 + Cumulative Return)1/N − 1
Where N is the number of years. For example, a 5-year cumulative return of 63.13%:

(1 + 0.6313)1/5 − 1 = (1.6313)0.2 − 1 = 0.1028 = 10.28%
This accounts for the effect of compounding — a 63.13% total return over 5 years is equivalent to 10.28% per year compounded, not simply 63.13% ÷ 5 = 12.63%.

About Default Values

Expected return (7.0%): A prudent central estimate aligned with the FCA's medium-to-high projection range. The FCA prescribes 2% (low), 5% (medium), and 8% (high) gross rates for pension illustrations. The fund's own long-term performance (estimated ~9.1% net, derived from the MSCI World GBP 37-year gross return of 9.74% minus the fund's 0.60% OCF) sits above this, but the fund underperforms its benchmark by ~1.3–1.5%/yr due to tracking error, and past returns may not persist. 7% balances historical evidence with forward-looking prudence.

Inflation (2.5%): Based on the post-Bank of England independence (1997–2025) CPIH average of ~2.3%, rounded up slightly to account for the BoE's persistent tendency to overshoot its 2% target. The full 1990–2025 CPIH average is higher (~2.8%) but is skewed by pre-independence high-inflation years that are less representative of the modern monetary policy environment.

Salary growth (3.5%): Reasonable lifetime average for a UK professional. Early-career growth (30s) is typically 4–6% per year due to promotions, tapering to 2–4% in mid-career and inflation-only adjustments later. The CoLA + 1% real raise option models this more precisely when the salary cap is enabled.

Salary cap (£150k / 15 years): Reflects the observation that salary growth is not exponential — most careers plateau. Beyond this point, only cost-of-living adjustments apply. Adjust based on your field and career trajectory.

Withdrawal rate (4%): Based on the "4% rule" from the Trinity Study (Cooley, Hubbard & Walz, 1998), which found that withdrawing 4% of a portfolio annually (adjusted for inflation) had a high probability of lasting 30+ years. Widely used as a retirement planning benchmark, though some argue 3.5% is more prudent for longer retirements.
